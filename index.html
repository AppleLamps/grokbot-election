The bottom line

If the polls are roughly right, Democrats are favored to win both chambers: about a 68% chance of a Senate majority and 93% for the House, riding a generic-ballot polling lead of D+8.3 (average as of Oct 18). Republicans' realistic path to holding both chambers requires the polls to be understating Republicans, as they did in 2016, 2020 and 2024. In that scenario, Republicans become Senate favorites (about 65%), but Democrats still win the House roughly 70% of the time. If polls instead miss in Democrats' favor, as in the 2025 off-year elections, it becomes a Democratic wave in both chambers.

Pick a polling-error scenario

Polls are never perfect, and their errors tend to point the same way in every race. Each scenario shifts that shared, nationwide error.

Why +3 one way and +6 the other?

Each scenario copies the size of a real, recent polling miss rather than mirroring the other. In presidential years polls overstated Democrats by about 3 points (2016: 3.0, 2020: 4.7, 2024: 2.9). In the 2025 off-year elections they overstated Republicans by about 6. Recent midterms were close to even (2018: R+0.5, 2022: D+0.8), which is the “unbiased” case. Averaged over all three Trump presidential years the miss was 3.8 points, while Trump-era off-year and special elections averaged only 1.3 points toward Republicans, so the 6-point scenario is best read as a tail case. Every scenario keeps a ±3-point, fat-tailed spread around its average miss, plus separate regional and Hispanic-voter error layers.

What's new in v2: eight more factors

We added eight factors that most often trip up polling-based forecasts. They largely cancel each other out. Herding-aware weighting (on its own) and expert House ratings pull Democrats down, while the likely-voter adjustment, a smaller incumbency edge, treating appointed senators as open seats, and candidate quality push Democrats up. Net, the headline odds moved by less than a point.

    How the model works

    1. Estimate each race. Senate: a weighted blend of the race's polling average and a fundamentals estimate (Cook PVI × 2 + the generic ballot (D+8.3 polling average as of Oct 18, plus a +0.4 likely-voter adjustment that is a model assumption, D+8.7 in total) + incumbency + candidate adjustments). Poll weight is cut for stale, partisan-sponsored, or herding-suspect polls. House: fundamentals (PVI + generic ballot + a 1.8-pt incumbency edge) blended with expert race ratings, across all 435 districts on the 2026 maps.
    2. Add correlated polling error. In every simulation, a fat-tailed national polling-error term shifts every race the same direction, plus shared errors by state, by Census region, and among Hispanic voters, on top of independent race-level noise. That is why polling misses tend to sweep the board instead of cancelling out.
    3. Simulate 100,000 elections per scenario and count who controls each chamber, including how independents caucus.
    4. Vary the polling-error assumption: centered at zero, a 3-point pro-Democratic polling miss (the 2016/2020/2024 pattern), or a 6-point pro-Republican miss (the 2025 pattern).

    !Honest limitations

    • Governor races use a simpler model than the Senate: forecaster ratings plus polls, no fundamentals or candidate terms, so they inherit the forecasters' judgment.
    • Snapshot as of October 10, 2026. Polls, candidates and ratings change; nothing here updates automatically.
    • The House model is still simple. It now blends in expert ratings, but has no district polls, district-level candidate data or fundraising, and uses each state's Hispanic share as a stand-in for district demographics on the new maps. It still sits a few seats above the pros' 224–234 range in the unbiased case.
    • Every coefficient is our assumption. The herding, demographic, candidate-quality, incumbency and ranked-choice settings are judgment calls informed by published research, not values fitted to past elections. The herding evidence comes from one source and is not proof.
    • Simplified, not a professional forecast. Several Senate races have little or no public polling.
    • The scenarios are “what if” lenses, not predictions of which polling miss will happen.
